Treatments for SIBO

Simple Chi Square Tests to Compare Efficacy of Rifaximin vs. Herbal Treatments for SIBO

Data originating from (Chedid, et al., 2014).

Treatment of patients with SIBO is often relegated to the off-label use of Rifaximin, which has a poor remission rate. Patients are increasingly interested in the use of alternative medicines to treat SIBO, which encourages the examination of their efficacy in comparison to current standards of care. Chedid et al. set out to examine the real-world effectiveness of herbal treatments vs. rifaximin in the remission of SIBO, as measured by repeat lactulose breath tests (LBT). The sample for this analysis consisted of EHRs from gastroenterology patients at a tertiary care gastroenterology practice.

Three hundred and ninety-six patients in were identified that met the inclusion criteria and did not meet the exclusion criteria, of which 104 completed a follow up LBT.

Patients were originally given two open-label treatment options based on their individual treatment preference; two 200 mg rifaximin tablets three times daily (TID), or two capsules twice daily of the commercial herbal preparations; Dysobocide and FC CIdal (Biotics Rsearch Laboratories, Rosenberg Texas) or Candibactin-AR and Candibactin BR (Metagenics, Inc, Aliso Viejo, California) for 4 consecutive weeks immediately followed by a follow-up LBT.

The primary outcome was the proportion of patients in each treatment regimen who had a negative post-treatment LBT.

Although the herbal treatments had a higher remission rate than Rifaximin, the unequal sample sizes make it unclear if there is a statistically significant advantage to using herbal treatments compared to Rifaximin. Using a Chi-Square Test of Homogenity:

\(H_{0} :\) The proportion of Rifaximin responders is equal to that of herbal treatment responders

\(H_{a} :\) Between the populations, the proportions are not equal

Based on the results of the Chi-Square test, it is clear that treatment with the herbal protocols was not statistically superior to that of treatment with Rifaximin.

So how do the odds of being a responder on herbal treatments differ from those of being on rifaximin?

Odds Ratio

Chedid et al. chose to adjust the odds of responding to either treatment by balancing covariates across their data set. In doing so, they obtained an odds ratio of 1.85 (C.I. of 0.77-4.41, p = .17). Not having access to the patient-level data to perform covariate adjustment, the expected odds ratio based on the un-adjusted data is as follows:

Without adjusting for covariates, the odds of responding to the herbal treatment regimine are 1.6 times greater than the odds of responding to Rifaximin. Though apparently greater, the increase in odds continues to reflect statistical non-significance.

A perhaps more intuitive understanding can be drawn from the risk ratio of responding to herbal treatments, compared to responding to rifaximin. This can be intuited as the ratio of the probability of responding to herbal treatments, compared to the probability of responding to rifaximin.

The probability of treatment naive patients responding to herbal treatments is 1.3 times greater than the probability of treatment naive patients responding to rifaximin, or there is a 30% increase in the relative risk of responding to herbal treatments compared to rifaximin. The wide confidence interval in this calculation once again confirms a non-statistically significant difference between treatment groups.

Importantly, of the 43 rifaximin non-responders, 24 were offered rescue treatment with herbal therapies. Fourteen of the 24 were only offered herbal treatments, and 10 were offered the choice of herbal treatments or triple antibiotics. Of the cohort soley offered herbal treatments, 8/14 (57.14%) had a negative follow-up LBT. Within the cohort offered the choice between treatments, 6/10 treated with triple antibiotics had a negative follow-up LBT (60%). The difference between follow up cohorts was non-significant.

Taken together, the data from Chedid et al.’s research suggests that the efficacy of certain herbal treatments for SIBO are not statistically different to treatment with the current standard of care antibiotic rifaximin. Furthermore, rescue therapy with herbal treatments or triple antibiotics for rifaximin non-responders appeared to be equally as efficacious. An examination of the adverse event profiles for both rifaximin and herbal treatments showed a bias towards fewer events in those treated with herbal treatments (6, including one case of anaphylaxis, for rifaximin vs. 1 for herbal treatments). With no significant statistical difference in effectiveness between Riffaximin and herbal treatments, and an initially positive safety profile, an investigation of the clinical significance of the treatment differences remains.
